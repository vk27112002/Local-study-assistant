GARCH Models for Volatility Modeling

Generalized Autoregressive Conditional Heteroskedasticity (GARCH) models are used to model time-varying volatility in financial time series, where periods of high volatility tend to cluster together.

The basic GARCH(1,1) model specifies the conditional variance as:
sigma_t^2 = omega + alpha * epsilon_(t-1)^2 + beta * sigma_(t-1)^2

Here, omega is a constant, alpha captures the reaction of volatility to recent shocks (the ARCH term), and beta captures the persistence of volatility (the GARCH term). For the model to be stationary, alpha + beta must be less than 1. Values of alpha + beta close to 1 indicate high volatility persistence, meaning shocks take a long time to decay.

An ARCH(1) model is a restricted case of GARCH(1,1) where beta = 0, meaning volatility depends only on the most recent squared shock and not on its own past values. A likelihood-ratio test comparing ARCH(1) against GARCH(1,1) can determine whether the additional GARCH term is statistically justified, or whether the simpler ARCH(1) specification is preferred by the data.

Extensions of the base model include:
- EGARCH, which models the log of variance and allows for asymmetric effects (leverage effect), where negative shocks increase volatility more than positive shocks of the same magnitude.
- GARCH-X, which incorporates exogenous variables (such as event dummies or macroeconomic indicators) into the variance equation.
- Markov-Switching GARCH, which allows model parameters to switch between distinct volatility regimes (e.g., calm vs turbulent markets).

Before fitting a GARCH model, analysts typically test for structural breaks (using tests like the Chow test or CUSUM test) to check whether a single set of parameters is appropriate across the full sample, or whether the series should be split into separate regimes.

Once fitted, GARCH models are commonly used to compute Value-at-Risk (VaR), a risk measure representing the maximum expected loss at a given confidence level over a given time horizon. Basel-style backtesting compares the number of actual VaR breaches against the expected number implied by the confidence level to assess model adequacy.
